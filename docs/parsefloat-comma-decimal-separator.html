Why parseFloat misreads comma decimals

parseFloat does not fail on numbers written with a comma decimal separator. It does something worse: it returns a wrong number.

3 min readUpdated October 15, 2026numberparrot 1.1.0

What parseFloat actually does#

parseFloat reads characters from the start of the string while they look like an English number, then stops and returns whatever it has. It never reports an error for the rest of the string.

Watch out

A wrong number is more dangerous than NaN: 1.234 passes every isNaN check and quietly ends up in your database, invoice or chart.

Number() is stricter, but not smarter#

Number rejects the whole string, which is safer, but it still only understands one format: a dot as the decimal separator and no grouping at all.

Why replace(",", ".") is not enough#

The most common answer online is to swap the comma for a dot. It works for "1,5" and breaks for everything else:

InputMeaningNumber(s.replace(",", "."))
1,51.5 (de-DE)1.5
1.234,561234.56 (pt-BR)NaN
1,2341234 (en-US)1.234
1 234,561234.56 (fr-FR)NaN
R$ 10,0010 (pt-BR)NaN

Removing every dot first (s.replace(/\./g, "").replace(",", ".")) fixes Brazilian input and silently corrupts English input, where the dot is the decimal separator. There is no character swap that is right for every locale, because the meaning of . and , depends on who wrote the number.

The fix#

Parse with the locale the number was written in. numberparrot gets the separators from Intl.NumberFormat and validates the whole string:
